Animal Health Economics · The Prevention Break-Even

“Prevention pays.”
Everyone says it. Almost nobody shows where it stops.

A preventive protocol only earns its cost above a certain disease incidence. That threshold is one division — and it is the most useful number in the business case.

Break-even incidence = programme cost per animal ÷ (efficacy × cost per case) Below that incidence the programme costs more than the disease it prevents. Above it, every point of incidence is margin. Net benefit per animal = (incidence × efficacy × cost per case) − programme cost.

Two things this changes in a real conversation. First, “what is your incidence?” stops being a clinical question and becomes the commercial one — it decides whether the protocol is an investment or a cost. Second, the honest answer in a low-incidence herd is that prevention does not pay on treatment savings alone; it has to be argued on variance, welfare, antimicrobial reduction or trade access. Those are real arguments. They are just not this one.
Method · Deterministic single-period model. Net benefit per animal = (annual incidence × efficacy × cost per case) − programme cost per animal; break-even incidence = programme cost ÷ (efficacy × cost per case); return = benefit ÷ cost − 1. Cost per case bundles treatment, labour, production loss, mortality and cull risk. The numbers above are an illustrative worked example, not evidence: incidence, efficacy and cost per case must come from your own records or published trials for the output to mean anything. Excluded by design: discounting, sub-clinical losses, variance and risk-aversion (prevention narrows the spread of outcomes, not just its mean), compliance drop-off and herd immunity — all of which move the answer in prevention's favour.
