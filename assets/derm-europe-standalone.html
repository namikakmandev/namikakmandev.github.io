Canine Dermatology Medicines — Europe

No published source measures this market in any single country. This assessment estimates it for 15 markets on one identical method: clinical rates taken from UK primary-care records, and medicine costs anchored on Polish retail prices then adjusted for each country's price level. All monetary values are estimates. Select any country below to see its own inputs and workings.

Estimated · 15 markets
€270–1,630M

Spent each year on medicines to treat skin problems in dogs, across the 15 markets in this panel. Central estimate €750M. The panel holds 74.1 million dogs — about 70% of Europe's estimated 106 million.

Read the range, not the point

The band is wide because two inputs are uncertain everywhere: how many dogs are treated in a year, and how much medicine a treated dog consumes. Individual country figures are less reliable than the total — errors partly cancel in aggregate and compound individually. Treat a single country's number as an order of magnitude, never as a forecast.

01How the estimate is built

Every country uses the same three inputs, multiplied together:

Only the first and third inputs change between countries. Dog populations come from national pet-food industry bodies. Medicine cost starts from Polish retail prices — 265 złoty (about €62) per treated dog — and is scaled by each country's Eurostat price level.

The one rule

Clinical rates are transferred between countries. Prices are not. How often dogs get skin disease is broadly stable across comparable dog populations. What treatment costs varies with local price levels. So the 8–18% rate is the same everywhere; the cost input changes country by country.

02The assumptions, in full

Four assumptions apply to every country in the panel. They are the whole basis of the estimate.

AssumptionWhat is assumedWhy, and how strong
1 · Treated share 8–18% of dogs are treated for a skin problem each year, central 12% From UK VetCompass primary-care records (skin disorders, 12.58% of dogs in a year). Applied to all countries. Weak outside the UK — no other country publishes an equivalent. Breed mix and climate vary.
2 · Medicine cost 265 zł (~€62) per treated dog in Poland, range 145–385 zł Built from Polish retail listings. A single episode costs 50–150 zł; a chronic case 1,000–2,500 zł a year. Weak — no source publishes an average, so the two are blended.
3 · Chronic share 10% of treated dogs are chronic cases on long-term medication Sets the blend in assumption 2. The single most influential input. At 5% the panel total falls by about a third; at 20% it rises by about a half.
4 · Price scaling Medicine cost scales with each country's general price level (Eurostat, EU27=100) Transparent and published. But it likely overstates the spread — medicines are traded goods sold by a few pan-European manufacturers, so their prices vary less between countries than haircuts do. See the sensitivity below.
Sensitivity — the rich/poor spread is probably too wide

Assumption 4 stretches per-dog medicine cost from €55 in Romania to €122 in Denmark, a 2.2× spread, because that is how general consumer prices differ. Medicines almost certainly vary less. Halving that dispersion lowers the panel central estimate from €750M to €649M — a 13% reduction, and the more likely figure of the two. The higher number is reported as the headline because it uses the published index without further adjustment.

04Does the method hold up?

One published figure exists at European level, from a commercial research house. Comparing against it is the only external check available.

A 1.4–1.6× gap against an unaudited vendor figure, with overlapping ranges, is reasonable agreement for a market nobody measures. It is the strongest evidence available that the method is sound.

The same vendor's country data fails its own test

That vendor also publishes a Germany figure. It is 22.0% of their own European total — but Germany has 10% of Europe's dogs, and at a price level of 109 you would expect about 11%. Their German number therefore carries 2.0× what its dog population and prices justify.

A second, independent test agrees: that figure implies €240–283 of medicine per treated German dog per year, which would require a simple episode to consume €150–211 of medicine. Retail prices in both countries contradict it. Published country-level figures in this category should not be relied on without checking them this way.

05What to do with this

Report

Quote the panel range, and say it is medicines only. €270–1,630M across 15 markets, central €750M. Counted with veterinary fees and tests the same treatment is roughly double. Published market reports use a wider definition again, adding flea and tick products and cats, and are not comparable.

Verify

Test one country against a real account. Poland works out at roughly €8,000 of dermatology medicine per veterinary practice per year across 7,600 practices. Anyone with distribution in a panel market can check the equivalent figure in an afternoon.

Priority

If buying data, buy the chronic-case share. Not another market-size report. It moves the panel total by roughly a third downward or a half upward across a plausible range — more than any other input, in every country at once.

Focus

Five markets are 72% of the panel. The UK, Germany, Italy, Spain and France together account for about €540M of the €750M central estimate. Precision in the remaining ten changes little.

Caution

Do not quote a single country figure as a forecast. Country numbers carry the full uncertainty of all four assumptions without the benefit of errors cancelling. The UK is the best-grounded because its disease rate is domestic; Poland is the most thoroughly worked.

Basis of estimates. All monetary figures are estimates; no source measures this market in any country. Measured inputs: dog populations from FEDIAF Facts & Figures (2022–23 data) and UK Pet Food (13.5M UK dogs, 2024); UK VetCompass primary-care prevalence (skin disorders 12.58% of dogs in one year); Polish retail medicine listings (oclacitinib 16mg, 46–90 zł per 20 tablets, 2026) and Polish veterinary consultation prices (100–250 zł, 2024–25); Eurostat comparative price levels for household final consumption, 2024 (EU27=100). Grand View Research Horizon figures for Europe and Germany are reported for comparison only and are not used in the estimate. Principal assumptions: the UK disease rate applied to every country, and 10% of treated dogs being chronic cases.

Calculation. Per country: dogs × {8%, 12%, 18%} treated × medicine cost per treated dog. Medicine cost = €61.6 (Poland, 265 zł ÷ 4.30) × country price level ÷ 72, with low and high scaled by 145/265 and 385/265. The Polish figure is a weighted average of a single episode (50–150 zł) and a chronic case (1,000–2,500 zł a year) at a 10% chronic share. EUR/PLN 4.30; USD/EUR 1.08. Confirmed vs approximate price levels. Confirmed from the Eurostat 2024 release: Denmark 143, Belgium 117, Germany 109, Italy 98, Spain 91, Czechia 88, Greece 86, Hungary 74, Poland 72, Romania 64. Approximate, marked on each country: United Kingdom ~117, France ~109, Sweden ~119, Austria ~120, Portugal ~88. A five-point error in a price level moves a country estimate by about 5%, which is immaterial against a range of this width. Limitations. Romania counts pet dogs only; its 0.6–1.0M street dogs are excluded, as they do not generate medicine sales. European veterinary prices have risen about 37% since 2015 and continue at roughly 6% a year against general inflation near 2%, so older inputs understate current spend.
