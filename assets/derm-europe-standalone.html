Canine Dermatology Medicines — 18 Markets

No published source measures this market in any single country. This assessment estimates it for 18 markets — 15 in Europe, plus the United States, Türkiye and Israel — on one identical method: clinical rates taken from UK primary-care records, and medicine costs anchored on Polish retail prices, adjusted to each country's price level. All monetary values are estimates. Select any country below to see its own inputs, workings and year-by-year path.

All 18 markets combined come to roughly $2.2bn a year at central estimates, dominated by the United States (~$1.3bn). The panel holds 74.1 million dogs — about 70% of Europe's estimated 106 million. Read the range, not the point

Two bands exist and they mean different things. The input band ($290–1,760M) is what the model produces when every input sits at its extreme simultaneously, in every country at once — a stress test, not a likely range. The reported band ($500–1,200M) trims it with two measured external anchors (section 06): audited revenues of the two dominant medicines put a hard floor under the market, and the published vendor figure — itself shown to be overstated — caps the top. Country figures inside the explorer keep their full input ranges. Individual country figures are less reliable than the total — errors partly cancel in aggregate and compound individually.

01How the estimate is built

Every country uses the same three inputs, multiplied together:

Dogspet dogs in the
country × 8–18%treated for a skin
problem each year × Costmedicine per
treated dog = Marketper year

Only the first and third inputs change between countries. Dog populations come from national pet-food industry bodies. Medicine cost starts from Polish retail prices — 265 złoty (about $67) per treated dog — and is scaled by each country's Eurostat price level.

The one rule

Clinical rates are transferred between countries. Prices are not. How often dogs get skin disease is broadly stable across comparable dog populations. What treatment costs varies with local price levels. So the 8–18% rate is the same everywhere; the cost input changes country by country.

02The assumptions, in full

Four assumptions apply to every country in the panel. They are the whole basis of the estimate.

But it likely overstates the spread — medicines are traded goods sold by a few pan-European manufacturers, so their prices vary less between countries than haircuts do. See the sensitivity below.
Sensitivity — the rich/poor spread is probably too wide

Assumption 4 stretches per-dog medicine cost from $59 in Romania to $132 in Denmark, a 2.2× spread, because that is how general consumer prices differ. Medicines almost certainly vary less. Halving that dispersion lowers the panel central estimate from $810M to $700M — a 13% reduction, and the more likely figure of the two. The higher number is reported as the headline because it uses the published index without further adjustment.

04The trend — as far back as data exists

What can and cannot be observed

No one has ever measured this market over time, so the market itself has no observable history — any long series of "the European dermatology medicines market" would be an invention. What does exist is the measured history of its drivers, and one series that comes close to the market itself: the maker of the two dominant medicines reports its dermatology revenue in audited filings. That is where the trend can honestly be read.

The market in its current shape is barely a decade old. Before 2014, chronic skin disease in dogs was managed with corticosteroids costing cents a day, and a small cyclosporine niche. Oclacitinib (Apoquel, launched 2014) and lokivetmab (Cytopoint, 2017) created the premium chronic-treatment segment that now carries most of the market's value. A trend line drawn through earlier years on today's definition would be meaningless — the products did not exist.

The underlying demand base grew steadily but far more slowly: Europe's dog population rose from about 73 million in 2010 to about 90 million in 2022 — roughly 1.7% a year. (FEDIAF's 2023 figure of ~104–106 million is largely a survey-coverage change, not fourteen million new dogs in one year, and is not treated as growth here.) Veterinary prices add another layer: up about 37% since 2015, still rising around 6% a year against general inflation near 2%.

What a decade of growth is made of

Applying today's method with 2014 inputs — fewer dogs, lower prices, and a chronic segment of roughly 2% on cheap steroids instead of 10% on modern therapies — the same 15-market panel would have been worth about $225M in 2014, against $810M today. What this means going forward

The largest growth engine — the one-off shift from steroids to modern therapies — is largely spent in Western Europe but not in the CEE markets, where the chronic-treatment segment is still building. If dogs keep growing at ~1.5%, prices at ~5%, and the therapy shift continues in the East, the panel grows at roughly 8–10% a year — doubling in seven to nine years. What would break this: loss of exclusivity on the two dominant molecules bringing generic JAK inhibitors, which would grow treated volume but shrink value.

Two implications for this study. First, the +4.5%/yr price driver in the modelled series is conservative against these measured rates — the series is more likely to run high than low on prices. Second, pet-owner inflation persistently outrunning general inflation is itself evidence of the therapy shift: owners are not just paying more per item, they are buying more advanced care. Where the two diverge most — Poland — is where the shift has furthest to run.

Visits per dog is deliberately not an input to the estimate — it predicts veterinary services volume, while medicine demand runs on treated dogs and therapy mix. It is listed as a watch item because a sustained rise in visits per dog in the CEE markets would be the earliest visible sign of the therapy-shift engine starting there — the main upside in this study.

05Beyond Europe — the United States, Türkiye and the Middle East

Three non-European markets are in the country list above with their own workings. They could not be less alike: the United States alone exceeds the whole European panel, while Türkiye and Israel together add about $15M — under 2% of it.

The United States — the strongest external check in the study

89.7M dogs (AVMA 2024) put the US estimate at $490–2,930M, central ~$1,340M. Uniquely, this figure can be tested head-on: the maker of the two dominant medicines earns most of its dermatology revenue in the US. At a 55–60% US share of the $1.65bn franchise, those two products alone account for $910–995M in the US — 71% of the central estimate here, leaving roughly $390M for every other skin medicine across 90M dogs. That is tight, and a second check agrees: US oclacitinib retails at 2–5× the Polish price, against the 1.9× general price-level scaler used here. Both checks say the US figure is more likely understated than overstated — the price-level rule, noted in section 02 as overstating the spread within Europe, understates it for the US, where medicine prices exceed general prices by an unusually wide margin.

The UAE, Saudi Arabia and the wider Gulf are declared out of scope rather than guessed at. No public dog-population figure exists for any of them: the UAE publishes a total pet-market value (~USD 400M, all species and services) but no dog count, and its municipal registration totals are not published; Saudi dog ownership is culturally limited and unrecorded. An estimate without a dog count would be invention. If these markets matter commercially, the municipal registries in Dubai and Abu Dhabi are the place to ask — the data exists, it is simply not public.

A caveat specific to Türkiye. Its general price level (~40 on EU=100) is both volatile — the lira's depreciation produced the largest price-level swing in the Eurostat programme between 2022 and 2024 — and probably the wrong scaler here: veterinary medicines are imported and priced closer to European levels than local services are. The Turkish estimate is therefore more likely understated than overstated, and carries the widest uncertainty in the study.

Narrowing the range — what the external anchors rule out

The raw input range ($290–1,760M) assumes every input sits at its extreme at once, in all fifteen countries simultaneously. Two measured anchors cut it down.

The floor. The two dominant premium medicines alone sell an estimated $300–450M in Europe (from their maker's audited $1,655M franchise, at a 40–45% international share with Europe roughly half of that), of which $220–360M falls inside this panel. A $290M total market would leave essentially nothing for every other skin medicine — antibiotics, antifungals, topicals, otitis preparations — across roughly eight million treated dogs. Adding a bare-minimum $20 of other medicines for simple episodes puts the hard floor near $350M, and the joint-extremes objection lifts the plausible floor to $500M.

The ceiling. The published vendor figure for Europe — which the two tests below show to be overstated — still implies at most $1,200–1,440M for this panel. Taking the bottom of that as the cap gives $1,200M.

Reported range: $500–1,200M, central $810M — a 2.4× spread instead of 6×. The trimming uses measured data only; the central estimate is unchanged.

The same vendor's country data fails its own test

That vendor also publishes a Germany figure. It is 22.0% of their own European total — but Germany has 10% of Europe's dogs, and at a price level of 109 you would expect about 11%. Their German number therefore carries 2.0× what its dog population and prices justify.

A second, independent test agrees: that figure implies $260–305 of medicine per treated German dog per year, which would require a simple episode to consume $160–230 of medicine. Retail prices in both countries contradict it. Published country-level figures in this category should not be relied on without checking them this way.

07What to do with this

Report

Quote the panel range, and say it is medicines only. $500–1,200M across 15 markets, central $810M (raw input extremes $290–1,760M, trimmed by the external anchors in section 06). Counted with veterinary fees and tests the same treatment is roughly double. Published market reports use a wider definition again, adding flea and tick products and cats, and are not comparable.

Verify

Test one country against a real account. Poland works out at roughly $8,600 of dermatology medicine per veterinary practice per year across 7,600 practices. Anyone with distribution in a panel market can check the equivalent figure in an afternoon.

Priority

If buying data, buy the chronic-case share. Not another market-size report. It moves the panel total by roughly a third downward or a half upward across a plausible range — more than any other input, in every country at once.

Focus

Five markets are 72% of the panel. The UK, Germany, Italy, Spain and France together account for about $585M of the $810M central estimate. Precision in the remaining ten changes little.

Caution

Do not quote a single country figure as a forecast. Country numbers carry the full uncertainty of all four assumptions without the benefit of errors cancelling. The UK is the best-grounded because its disease rate is domestic; Poland is the most thoroughly worked.
