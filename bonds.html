Five per cent. The bond market has repriced the state.

On 15 September 2026 the ten-year US Treasury closed at 5.00%, a level last seen at the close in July 2007. Five days earlier the thirty-year closed at 5.37%, its highest since 2004. The move is not American alone: in August the ten-year yield stood at its highest since 2008 in Britain and France, since 2011 in Germany and since 1996 in Japan. Of the 0.82 points the US ten-year added this year, 0.69 is real yield and 0.13 inflation compensation; the model that splits it another way gives 0.39 to the term premium and 0.43 to expected policy. Sixty years of US data say the deficit does not time the yield. This year's six countries say it sets the level.

A data companion to The Economist's piece of 16 September 2026, "Soaring bond yields, gaping deficits and towering debts: what could go wrong?" It does not summarise the article; it checks what the public series say. Every figure below is recomputed from the committed data files by scripts/bond_study.py; the sources and the caveats are listed at the end. Part two follows the yields into the debt: who paid it down, the interest bill against revenue, and how fast a higher yield reaches the stock.

Six governments, one directionTen-year government bond yields, monthly averages, 1990 to August 2026. OECD and Federal Reserve series via FRED.

The chart is the thirty-year decline in the price of government borrowing, and its end. Every line falls from 1990 to 2020, Japan first and furthest; every line then turns in 2021 and has not come back. What is new in 2026 is not the direction but where the levels have got to: the British and French ten-year yields are back at 2008, the German at 2011, the Japanese at 1996. The US and Italy are only at late-2023 levels on a monthly average, because both spiked in the autumn of 2023; the US daily close has since gone past that spike, which the next section shows.

What the US move is made ofThe Treasury curve since 2021, weekly points, and the two ways of splitting the ten-year yield, 31 December 2025 to 15 September 2026.

The two-year yield rose more than the ten-year this year, 1.20 points against 0.82, so the curve flattened from 0.71 to 0.33 points between two and ten years. That is the shape of a market pricing central-bank tightening, not only of one worried about supply thirty years out. The ten-year's 0.82 splits two ways in the data. Against the inflation-protected bond, 0.69 of it is a higher real yield and only 0.13 is inflation compensation, which sits at 2.38%: the market did not reprice inflation, it repriced the real cost of money. In the New York Fed's model, 0.39 is a higher term premium and 0.43 is higher expected short rates. The term premium, at 0.96 points, is the highest of the post-2010 era but ordinary by the standard of the 1990s, when the same model put it above two.

The debts and the deficits behind itGeneral government gross debt, per cent of GDP, 2000 to 2025 actual and IMF projections to 2031. IMF World Economic Outlook.

The IMF's numbers are the ones the article's title refers to, and they separate the six countries cleanly. The United States runs the largest deficit of the group, 6.8% of GDP in 2025, and is the only one the IMF projects to keep it there: 7.4% in 2031, with gross debt climbing from 124% to 142% of GDP. Britain and France are projected to narrow theirs, to 1.6% and 2.9%, which is a plan rather than a fact. Italy's debt is flat at 137%. Germany's rises from 63% to 74% as its spending rules loosen. Japan's, at 207%, is the highest and is falling, with a deficit of 1.1%. The dashed segments are projections and should be read as the IMF's assumptions, not as data.

imf-weo: GGXWDG_NGDP, general government gross debt, per cent of GDP. Actuals to 2025, projections after (dashed). The US figure is general government and gross, so it is about 25 points above the federal debt held by the public used below.

Washington's interest bill, and where it driftsUS federal net interest and the deficit, per cent of GDP, fiscal years 1962 to 2025. Debt held by the public, quarterly to Q1 2026.

Net interest took 3.15% of GDP in fiscal 2025, the most since 1991, on debt held by the public of 98.7% of GDP. Divide one by the other and the Treasury paid an average of 3.2% on its stock last year. The stock reprices only as it matures, but the direction is arithmetic: if the whole of it eventually carried today's ten-year yield of 5.00%, interest would take 4.9% of GDP, with the debt ratio unchanged. That is not a forecast. It is the level the bill drifts towards if nothing else moves, and it is larger than the entire 2025 deficit excluding interest. The deficit itself was 5.8% of GDP in fiscal 2025, which is where it has sat, outside the pandemic, since 2023.

Does the deficit time the yield? Sixty years say no.The US ten-year yield, annual mean, against the federal deficit and the debt, fiscal years 1966 to 2025.

The claim in the air is that deficits are pushing yields up. Tested through time, on the one country with sixty years of both series, it fails in the way it usually does. In levels the yield and the deficit are barely related, and once the sample is discounted for how slowly both move there are about ten independent observations, not sixty. In year-to-year changes the relationship is real and it is negative: a year in which the deficit widened by a point was a year in which the yield fell. That is recessions, when the deficit opens and the central bank cuts. The debt level against the yield is a textbook spurious correlation: r = −0.70 and an effective sample of two. Across this year's six countries, though, the picture reverses: the countries with the biggest 2025 deficits carry the highest yields in August 2026, and the debt level is if anything inversely related, because Japan has the most debt and the lowest yield. Six points cannot be tested. They can be read: the deficit does not set the yield's timing, and on this year's evidence it does sort the level.

What to do with this

SupportedA Turkish company with dollar or euro debt
Budget 2027 on 5% dollar money and 3 to 4% euro money, and refinance nothing on the assumption that yields go back to where they were in 2021.
What would change it: the ten-year Treasury closing below 4.5% for a full month. The daily series in us-treasury-daily is the thing to watch, not the monthly average.
Read as arithmeticA lira saver weighing a deposit against a Treasury
A three-month lira deposit paid 44.4% in September 2026 against 5.0% on a ten-year Treasury: the comparison is your view of the lira's fall over the period, not the yield. Five per cent is the highest risk-free dollar rate since 2007, and it is a real 2.6% after inflation compensation.
What would change it: the policy rate, now 37.0%, being cut faster than the lira depreciates. tr-rates prints both monthly.
Not supported as statedAnyone writing that "deficits are pushing yields up"
Say which claim you mean. As a timing claim it fails: through sixty years, wider US deficits went with lower yields. As a cross-section it holds this year: the biggest deficits carry the highest yields. Quote the second, and quote it as six points.
What would change it: a country in the group cutting its deficit and seeing its yield fall relative to the others within a year. France and Britain are the ones the IMF projects to try.

Read with care

  • Monthly against daily. The six-country chart uses monthly averages, so "highest since 2008" is a monthly-average statement; the US daily close reached 5.00% on 15 September 2026 and the monthly average for September will read lower. The two must not be mixed in one sentence.
  • The term premium is a model. The ACM term premium is the New York Fed's estimate of the part of the yield that is not expected short rates. It is revised when the model is re-estimated and can be negative. The real-yield split uses market prices (TIPS) and is not a model, but TIPS carry their own liquidity premium.
  • Two definitions of debt. The IMF's general government gross debt (124% of GDP for the US in 2025) includes state and local government and intragovernmental holdings; the federal debt held by the public (98.7%) excludes both. The interest arithmetic uses the second because that is what the interest is paid on.
  • Projections. The WEO file does not mark where actuals end; this page treats 2025 as the last actual year and draws 2026 to 2031 dashed. They are the IMF's fiscal assumptions and are revised twice a year.
  • The tests. Sixty annual points of two slow-moving series. The effective sample after the lag-1 autocorrelation discount is printed beside every correlation, and p_adj is read at that size. The changes test is the only one that clears it, and its sign is negative. The six-country cross-section is not a test and carries no p.
  • Not measured. Who holds the bonds, central-bank balance sheets, issuance by maturity, the oil price and the Middle East war that the press cites as this month's trigger. This page says what the yields, debts and deficits are; it does not say why September was the month.
