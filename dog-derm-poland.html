How big is dog dermatology in Poland? A number nobody publishes, built from public data.

Ask a research vendor for the canine dermatology market in Poland and you get a paywall, or a European figure with Poland buried inside it. No public source measures it directly. So this builds it — from free, checkable data, borrowing what can honestly be borrowed from four reference markets under one strict rule. Three independent methods land at €90–160 million a year for clinical dermatology. But the more useful finding is what the study ran into on the way: the definition of "dermatology market" moves the answer by 2.2× — more than every uncertain input combined.

Every euro figure here is modelled — there is no meter on this market. What is measured is the scaffolding: dog counts, disease prevalence, price levels, the vet-market ceiling and Germany's published derm figure.

1. Borrowing from other markets — the rule that makes it legitimate

Poland has no domestic canine-dermatology data at all: no prevalence study, no market figure. The only way forward is to borrow from markets that do. That is defensible, but only if you split your variables in two — because they do not travel equally.

The transfer rule

Rates transfer. Money does not. Disease prevalence, incidence, chronicity and breed risk are biology — a dog's skin does not know it is in Poland. Price per consult, spend per dog and treatment penetration scale with income, veterinary price level and insurance, all of which differ sharply. So: borrow the clinical rates, anchor every złoty locally.

Applied here, that means prevalence comes from the UK, the market-size cross-check comes from Germany, and every price input is Polish. The scaling factors that move German money into Polish conditions are themselves public:

Pet insurance penetration is the sharpest scaling factor. It is a direct proxy for treatment-seeking: an uninsured owner defers the second follow-up and declines the referral. Poland under 10% against Germany's ~20% means Polish dogs generate fewer billed episodes per case, not merely cheaper ones.

2. The prevalence ladder — four numbers that are not the same number

Before borrowing a rate, you have to check what it measures. Four published prevalence figures are available across the reference markets, and they range over two orders of magnitude — not because dogs differ, but because the objects differ:

Sweden's 0.17% and the UK's 12.58% differ by 74×, and neither is wrong. One counts insurance claims for a single diagnosis; the other counts every dog with any skin problem. Quote them in the same sentence and the analysis is finished before it starts.

Sweden earns its place in the panel precisely by not supplying the rate. Its data quality is the best available — near-universal insurance means near-complete records — but it measures a narrow diagnosis. It sets a floor for chronic atopic cases, and it proves why the ladder has to be checked before anything is borrowed.

A strict three-way intersection would give €110–118M. That is not reported as the answer — these are scenario ranges, not confidence intervals, and intersecting them would manufacture precision the inputs cannot support.

A revision, stated plainly. The first version of this study reported €70–150M with a point near €110M, using methods 1 and 2 only. Method 3 shares no inputs with either and lands higher, at €110–188M. The central band is therefore revised up to €90–160M, point ≈ €120M. The likeliest reason the bottom-up route ran low: it counts vet-attended episodes, so it misses medicated shampoos, supplements and topicals bought off a shelf with no consult.

5. The finding that matters — scope beats precision

Run the same Germany transfer without stripping antiparasitics — that is, using the definition commercial vendors actually publish — and Poland comes out at €201–343M. Same source, same country, same year, same arithmetic. Only the definition changed.

Scope B's lower bound is deliberately halved from the €201M transfer result, because vendors disagree with each other by roughly 2× on the same global market (Grand View: USD 17.4bn for 2024; Future Market Insights: USD 9.7bn for 2025). Scope B is therefore less certain than Scope A despite looking more official — its uncertainty is definitional and vendor-driven, nothing to do with Poland.
Whether flea-and-tick preventatives count as "dermatology" changes the answer by more than prevalence and spend uncertainty put together. Fix the definition before arguing about the number — and never compare your figure to a vendor's without checking which of these two things theirs is.

6. Who delivers it — the supply side needs no modelling

One part of this market is simply published. The National Chamber of Veterinarians' register is a free, complete list of every licensed animal-treatment establishment in Poland — roughly 7,600 facilities and ~10,000 vets, mostly single-vet clinics, concentrated in Mazovia (~1,691 businesses), Greater Poland, Silesia and Lower Silesia. Countable by voivodeship, derm-focused practices identifiable by hand. For sizing a sales territory or a referral network, that register is the asset — no vendor required.

What to do with this

  1. Quote the scope, always. "Canine dermatology in Poland is €90–160M/yr on a clinical definition, or €100–340M on the vendor definition that includes antiparasitics." A number without its scope is not comparable to anything. What would change it: a Polish prevalence figure replacing the UK proxy could move the clinical band by a third in either direction.
  2. Buy the prevalence, not the market size. Every method here is more sensitive to prevalence and spend-per-dog than to any market total. A Polish or CEE prevalence study is worth more than another vendor report — and it is the input no vendor sells well.
  3. If a real investment rides on it, buy Kynetec. VetTrak / PetTrak (which absorbed Vetnosis) is the only source that measures Polish derm sales and patient visits through the vet channel, monthly. Five-figure custom quote — justified for a market-entry or M&A call, not for a slide. Ask first whether your organisation already licenses it.
  4. Treat any single vendor number as ±100%. Two reputable publishers differ by 2× on the same global market. Triangulate or discount accordingly — and pull the free national vet register before commissioning anything paid.

Known limits. EU veterinary prices rose ~37% since 2015 and still run ~6%/yr against ~2% general inflation — figures older than about two years understate current spend. The top-down envelope captures clinic-channel spend only and leans low. Vendor country figures are unaudited and mutually inconsistent by ~2× at global level. No deflator is applied: all figures are a single recent year (2023–2026), not a time series. The UK→Poland prevalence transfer remains the load-bearing assumption of the whole study.
