STUDY ADDENDUM · HERD STRUCTURE · EU AND TÜRKİYE · JULY 2026

Europe lost 2.4 million dairy cows — and produced more milk.

The main study showed producer margins at a 55-year high while herds sat near record lows. This addendum asks what the shrinking herd actually is. It is not a shrinking industry: it is consolidation into fewer, higher-yielding, more valuable animals — and the money each animal carries grew faster than the herd shrank. That combination sets a different price position in every market. Eurostat livestock survey, farm milk statistics and economic accounts for agriculture; 27 EU member states plus Türkiye, 2015–2025.

THE BOTTOM LINE

  1. Fewer cows, more milk. Across 27 EU member states the dairy herd fell 11.2% between 2015 and 2024 while milk production rose 5.9%. Output per cow rose 19.2%, from 6,810 to 8,120 kg.
  2. The customer count and the customer value move in opposite directions. A base measured in head is shrinking about 1% a year. The same base measured in output per animal is growing about 2% a year.
  3. Beef shows the same direction, a smaller step. EU and Türkiye carcass weight per head rose from 288 to 296 kg, so slaughter tonnage fell only 1.5% while head slaughtered fell 4.2%.
  4. Türkiye is a different animal, literally. Carcass weight per head rose 29.5%, from 250 to 324 kg — the largest structural change of any market in this data.
  5. The value pool grew while the herd shrank — so head count is the wrong basis for price. Real cattle and milk output per animal rose in 26 of 27 markets. Combined with herd change, the real value pool grew in 24 of them: Poland +67%, Germany +18% despite losing 17% of its cattle, France just +3%.
  6. Two Europes on the replacement pipeline. Poland is rebuilding, with breeding heifers up 28% and the highest replacement ratio in the EU. The Netherlands, Denmark and Hungary cut theirs by roughly a third.

1 · THE HERD IS NOT THE MARKET

The main study reported the EU herd a third below its 1980 peak and read that as a shrinking customer base. Indexed against each other, the three lines say something different. Cattle numbers and dairy cow numbers fall together; milk output rises through the whole period. The gap between the falling and the rising line is the entire story of this addendum.

Milk per cow is the mechanism, and it is not a rounding effect. In Italy yield per cow rose 34.6%, in Poland 31.9%, in Lithuania 31.6%, in Germany 23.4%. Only Hungary (+0.5%), Bulgaria (+2.8%) and Romania (+5.9%) stood still.

3 · WHO IS REBUILDING, WHO IS LIQUIDATING

Yield says what happened. The replacement ratio says what happens next. A heifer kept back to breed is a producer betting on calves later instead of taking cash now; heifers sent to slaughter are the opposite bet. The change in that ratio is the closest thing in public data to a forward view of each national herd.

Pipeline growing Pipeline shrinking

Poland is the outlier that matters — Greece and Croatia move more but carry a twelfth of the cattle. Its herd grew 7.4% while its breeding pipeline grew 28.1%, taking its replacement ratio from 40.9 to 57.7 — the highest in the EU. That is a market building capacity while its neighbours run theirs down. The Netherlands (−34.4% breeding heifers) and Denmark (−33.7%) are doing the reverse, and Hungary grew its herd 6.0% while cutting breeding stock 31.5%, which is growth with nothing behind it.

4 · TÜRKİYE

Türkiye is the only market in this data whose herd is growing — up 20.2% between 2015 and 2024 — and the only one whose animals changed shape this fast. Carcass weight per head rose from 250 to 324 kg, +29.5%. A Turkish animal in 2024 yields nearly a third more meat than the same animal a decade earlier.

What this does not settle. Türkiye's replacement ratio fell from 47.0 to 41.5 over the same period — the steepest deterioration of any large herd here, worse than France or Germany. Breeding stock grew 11% in absolute terms while the herd grew 25%, so the herd is expanding faster than it is breeding its own replacements. That pattern is consistent with growth sourced partly from outside the domestic herd. No trade series exists in any source wired into this project, so the question stays open — we can show the gap, not its cause.

5 · THE VALUE POOL, AND WHAT IT PRICES

Herd change alone is a volume statement. Multiply it by the real change in output value per animal and you get the only number that should drive a price position: how much money the cattle and milk business in each market is actually worth now versus 2015, in constant euro. Twenty-two of these markets lost cattle. Twenty-four of them still grew their value pool.

Türkiye sits outside this table. Eurostat publishes no economic accounts for Türkiye, so there is no output-value figure and therefore no value pool. What is measurable is volume and physical output: the herd grew 20.2% and carcass weight per head rose 29.5%. That supports a volume-led position, not a value-led one — and the falling replacement ratio means the volume itself is not yet self-sustaining. Any Turkish price argument built on value per animal would need TurkStat data this project does not have.

Two consequences that cut across all four positions:

  • A single European price move is four different decisions. The spread between Poland's +67% pool and France's +3% is not a rounding difference — the same percentage increase is growth-funded in one market and margin-funded in the other.
  • Forecasting revenue off head count understates every market except Bulgaria. Head fell in 22 of 27; value rose in 24 of 27. A plan indexed to animals has been forecasting the wrong direction for nine years.

7 · 2027 — MODELLED, NOT MEASURED

Everything above this line is published statistics. Everything in this section is a projection, and the two should never be quoted together without saying which is which. Two years out, one half of the answer is close to already determined and the other half is not.

The volume half is nearly fixed. A cow calving in 2027 is a heifer standing in a shed today, so 2027 head counts are largely set by animals already alive. Carrying each country's 2015–2025 rate forward two years gives a narrow band: from −3.8% (Germany, Netherlands) to +1.5% (Hungary) against 2025.

The value half is not fixed at all, and it is currently elevated. Real output value per head in 2025 sits above its own ten-year average in 26 of the 27 markets — by 10.4% in Finland, 20.7% in France, 30.1% in Germany, 45.2% in Poland and 54.9% in Estonia. Bulgaria alone sits below its mean, by 3.7%. The main study found producer margins at a 55-year peak that has reversed five times out of five. This is the same fact seen from the revenue side.

  1. Scenario A — the level holds. Real value per head stays at its 2025 level. The 2027 pool then moves only with the herd: −3.8% to +1.5%.
  2. Scenario B — the cycle turns. Real value per head returns to that market's own 2015–2025 average. Not an invented haircut — each country's own decade mean. The 2027 pool then lands between −36.6% (Estonia) and +3.9% (Bulgaria, the one market currently below its mean). Across the markets above a million head the band is −30.3% to −16.1%.
A · level holds B · reverts to decade mean

The width of each bar is the honest uncertainty. It is not evenly distributed: the markets with the strongest recent growth carry the widest downside, because their premium over their own decade average is largest. Poland, the best market on every backward-looking measure on this page, carries the largest premium of any major market — its 2025 value per head is 45.2% above its ten-year mean. Estonia's is larger still at 54.9%, on a herd of 232,000 head. Bulgaria is the single exception in the other direction: it is 3.7% below its mean, so a turning cycle would lift it rather than cut it — the one market where Scenario B is the better outcome.

Read across the table and the instruction is the same everywhere, with different urgency: 2027 volume can be committed to, 2027 value cannot. Where a customer wants a multi-year price, the volume side is a fair thing to fix and the value side is not.

What would falsify this. Scenario B assumes the current elevation is cyclical. If the rise in value per head is structural — permanently smaller herds meeting steady demand — then reversion never comes and Scenario A understates 2027. The evidence for treating it as cyclical is the main study's five-for-five record of peaks reversing, which is five observations, not a law. Both scenarios are shown for exactly that reason.

10 · LIMITATIONS

  • The United States is not in this addendum. No equivalent per-category inventory is wired up, so every figure on this page is EU and Türkiye only.
  • Milk yield here is production ÷ dairy cows, not a herd-book yield. It moves when the cow definition moves, and it does not adjust for fat or protein content.
  • Carcass weight per head is derived as slaughter tonnage ÷ head slaughtered × 1,000, because Eurostat publishes the ready-made ratio only for the most recent year.
  • EU non-dairy cows read 10,082.27 for 2024 and 10,082.29 for 2025 — twenty head apart across a full year in a ten-million-head population. The 2025 value is provisional or carried forward, so it is excluded from anything on this page.
  • Small markets are noise at this resolution. Cyprus (84,000 head) and Malta (13,000) move in percentages that are not comparable to France or Germany, and are shown but never ranked on.
  • Output value is nominal euro, deflated by euro-area HICP. For the eight markets outside the euro, Eurostat converts at market exchange rates, so their value figures carry an FX component that no deflator removes. Poland, Czechia, Denmark, Sweden, Hungary, Romania and Bulgaria should be read with that in mind.
  • The value pool is cattle and milk output only — Eurostat items AM111000 and AM121000. It is the producer's revenue, not our addressable market, and it excludes every other species on the same farm.
  • Veterinary spend was examined and left out of the pricing argument. Eurostat's veterinary line (AM205000) covers all livestock, so dividing it by cattle numbers moves whenever a country's pig or poultry sector moves. The apparent result — the veterinary share of cattle and milk output falling in most large markets — cannot be attributed to cattle, and is not used here.
  • No causal claim. Nothing here explains why yields rose — genetics, feed, consolidation of farms and exit of small herds all plausibly contribute, and this data separates none of them.
