STUDY ADDENDUM · HERD STRUCTURE AND 2027 PRICING · EU AND TÜRKİYE · JULY 2026

Europe lost 2.4 million dairy cows and produced more milk. Our line did not follow the money.

The main study showed producer margins at a 55-year high while herds sat near record lows. This addendum takes the herd apart by country and asks what replaced the missing animals — and whether the answer licenses a price move. It does not, yet. Herd and breeding stock 2015–2025; milk, yield and output value 2015–2024; Eurostat livestock survey, farm milk statistics and economic accounts for agriculture.

THE BOTTOM LINE

  1. Fewer cows, more milk. Across 27 EU member states the dairy herd fell 11.2% between 2015 and 2024 while milk production rose 5.9%. Milk per dairy cow rose 19.2%, from 6,810 to 8,120 kg.
  2. Producer revenue per animal rose far faster than our line did. On a consistent 27-country panel, real cattle and milk output per head of cattle rose 31.8% between 2015 and 2024. The real Eurostat veterinary and medicines line per head rose 2.3%, from €96.7 to €98.9. The producer's wallet grew; the animal-health share of it did not.
  3. And most of the wallet growth is price, not structure. Two thirds of the real value-pool increase lands after 2021: +6.1% over 2015–2021, +12.6% over 2021–2024. Real milk value per tonne went from €419 to €514 while milk volume rose 5.9%.
  4. The volume half of 2027 is already determined; the value half is not. A cow calving in 2027 is a heifer alive today, so 2027 head counts sit in a narrow band. Output value per head is at a cyclical high the main study says has reversed five times out of five.
  5. The breeding pipeline splits Europe, and only three markets are truly rebuilding. Breeding stock rose in Greece (+29.6%), Poland (+28.1%) and Ireland (+3.2%) and nowhere else; three further markets show a rising ratio only because their cow herds fell faster. The Netherlands cut its by 34.4%, Denmark 33.7%, Hungary 31.5%. That is the only forward-looking signal here that is measured rather than modelled.

1 · THE HERD IS NOT THE MARKET

The main study reported the EU herd a third below its 1980 peak and read that as a shrinking customer base. Indexed against each other, the three lines say something different. Cattle numbers and dairy cow numbers fall together; milk output rises through the whole period.

Cattle, all Dairy cows Milk produced

Milk per cow is the mechanism, and it is not a rounding effect. Among the large markets, yield per cow rose 34.6% in Italy, 31.9% in Poland, 23.4% in Germany; the steepest rises of all are Croatia at 44.4% and Latvia at 41.4%, on much smaller herds. Four markets stood still: Hungary and Malta (+0.5%), Bulgaria (+2.8%) and Romania (+5.9%).

2 · WHERE THE YIELD WENT

Every bar below moves right. The question is how far each market still has to travel — a Romanian cow at 3,540 kg and an Estonian cow at 11,353 kg are not the same customer. Ireland sits low at 5,871 kg on a grass-based system; whether that is a gap to close or a deliberate trade-off is not something this data can settle.

2015 2024
Türkiye has the largest yield gap in this study and cannot be plotted above. Eurostat's Turkish farm milk series covers 2003–2009 and 2020–2024 only, so there is no 2015 base and no decade change. On the years that do exist, Turkish yield runs 3,614 kg/cow in 2020 and 3,345 kg in 2024 — the lowest of all 28 markets, below Romania and Bulgaria, in the one market whose herd is growing. That combination is the largest single opportunity visible anywhere in this data, and it is the one this page can say least about.

3 · WHO IS REBUILDING, WHO IS RUNNING THE HERD DOWN

Yield says what happened. The replacement ratio says what happens next: a heifer kept back to breed is a producer betting on calves later instead of taking cash now. The ratio must be read with the absolute heifer count beside it — a ratio can rise simply because the cow herd collapsed faster than the breeding stock did.

Pipeline growing Pipeline shrinking

Only three of the six rising ratios are real rebuilding. Breeding stock actually grew in Greece (+29.6%), Poland (+28.1%) and Ireland (+3.2%) — nowhere else. The other three rose on a collapsing denominator: Croatia's ratio gained 10.2 points while its breeding heifers fell 5.5% and its cow herd fell 25.7%; Lithuania gained 4.8 points with breeding stock down 12.5%; Austria gained 3.9 points on a stock that was flat to slightly down (−0.4%). Those three are marked on the chart. At the other end the Netherlands (−34.4%), Denmark (−33.7%) and Hungary (−31.5%) are cutting breeding stock outright, and their ratios fall too — no ambiguity there.

4 · TÜRKİYE

Türkiye is the largest market with a growing herd, not the only one — Cyprus (+40.5%), Poland (+7.4%), Hungary (+6.0%), Greece and Czechia (+2.3%) also grew between 2015 and 2024. In absolute terms Türkiye's increase is far the biggest at +2.86m head.

Two breaks that the headline hides. The Turkish herd goes 14,222k in 2016 to 16,105k in 2017 — a 13.2% jump in one year, with no economic event behind it. That single step is two thirds of the entire "+20.2% since 2015," and it is almost certainly a coverage or methodology change. Measured from 2017 the herd is up 5.5%, and 2024 sits 6.4% below the 2020 peak — which is how the main brief describes Türkiye. Carcass weight per head tells the same story: 250 kg in 2015 to 313 kg in 2020, then flat — 313, 304, 311, 324, and 320 in 2025. The structural change happened, and it finished five years ago. Any comparison spanning 2016–2017 should be avoided.

Türkiye's replacement ratio fell from 47.0 to 41.5: breeding stock grew 11.0% while the herd grew 25.3%, so the herd is expanding faster than it breeds its own replacements. That is consistent with growth sourced partly from outside the domestic herd. No trade series exists in any source wired into this project, so the cause stays open.

5 · THE VALUE POOL — AND WHAT IT IS MADE OF

Multiply herd change by the real change in output value per animal and you get the money the cattle and milk business in each market is worth now versus 2015. Twenty-two markets lost cattle; twenty-four still grew their pool. But before that number is used for anything, it has to be taken apart.

Value pool grew Value pool shrank

Most of this is a price spike, not a structural gain. Split at 2021, the EU real pool grew 6.1% over 2015–2021 and 12.6% over 2021–2024 — two thirds of a decade's growth in three years. Real milk value per tonne went from €419 in 2015 to €457 in 2021 to €514 in 2024, while volume rose only 5.9% across the whole period. Yield rose smoothly; value per head stepped in 2022. A pool built mostly on unit price is not the same asset as one built on volume, and it reverses differently.

6 · THE LINK THIS PAGE CANNOT PROVE

The tempting conclusion — the producer earns far more per animal, therefore there is room to price — requires a link from producer revenue to animal-health spend. The only series in this project that proxies that link says the opposite.

Output value per head Veterinary line per head

Over 2015–2024 real output value per head rose 31.8%, from €1,261 to €1,661. The real veterinary line per head rose 2.3%, from €96.7 to €98.9. In aggregate the real veterinary line fell 7.3% while real output rose 19.5% — the herd shrank faster than the spend per animal grew.

This is the main study's own EU finding, and it must be stated here. The brief reported that European veterinary spend per head "has been flat for twenty years and does not respond to the cycle (r = −0.12)," and concluded EU revenue is defensive rather than cyclical. Nothing in this addendum overturns that. Producer revenue is not evidence of willingness to pay us, and any pricing argument that leans on the value pool alone is assuming a link that two decades of data do not show. The honest reading of the gap between +31.8% and +2.3% is that it is either an uncaptured opportunity or proof that this wallet does not open for animal health — and this data cannot tell you which.

7 · WHAT THE HERD DATA DOES LICENSE ON PRICE

Strip out everything that depends on the unproven link and one instruction survives, because it rests on head counts rather than on value: 2027 volume can be committed to, 2027 value cannot. A cow calving in 2027 is a heifer standing in a shed today.

The usable number is therefore not a recommended increase — this data cannot produce one, because it contains neither our costs nor our realised prices. It is price-to-standstill: the per-animal price change needed for 2027 revenue to match 2025, if our revenue scales with head count. Every market, in section 8.

Read it as a floor, not a recommendation. Germany and the Netherlands need +4.0% per animal simply to stand still, and both have shrinking pipelines, so their herd projections are upper bounds. Poland, Hungary, Spain, Czechia, Bulgaria and Cyprus get revenue growth from volume alone. The assumption that decides this table. Price-to-standstill assumes our revenue is proportional to head count. If our revenue is weighted toward high-yielding dairy animals rather than spread evenly across cattle, the number is different — because the mix is shifting toward exactly those animals. Testing that needs our own sales data by market and species, which is not in this study.

9 · 2027 — MODELLED, NOT MEASURED

Everything above is published statistics. Everything in this section is a projection. Herd 2025→2027 uses each country's 2015–2025 rate carried forward two years: −3.8% (Germany, Netherlands) to +1.5% (Hungary), with Cyprus an outlier at +7.5% on 84,000 head.

The value side is elevated, but "above its own mean" proves less than it appears: on a trailing eleven-year window that statistic has been true for 24–26 of 27 markets every year since 2022, and any rising series sits above the mean of a window ending on it. The sharper measure is distance from the pre-spike 2015–2019 average: Poland +70.1%, Belgium +59.3%, Ireland +58.0%, Germany +48.8%, France +34.1%.

  1. A — the level holds. Real value per head stays at 2025. The pool moves only with the herd: −3.8% to +7.5%.
  2. B — reverts to the 2015–2025 mean. Arithmetic mean of real value per head in constant 2025 euro, 2015–2025 inclusive — eleven observations, including 2025 itself, which drags the anchor up and understates the fall. Pool −36.6% (Estonia) to +3.9% (Bulgaria).
  3. C — reverts to the pre-spike 2015–2019 mean. Five observations, none of them spike years. Pool −49.2% to +10.0%; Poland −40.5%, Germany −35.3%, Ireland −37.0%.
A · level holds B · 2015–2025 mean C · pre-spike mean
This method has been backtested and it failed. Run identically on data to end-2022 — eleven-year mean, ten-year CAGR, two-year horizon — and compared with what 2024 actually did: Italy landed at +5.6% against a Scenario A of −0.7%; Spain +6.6% against +2.1%. Reality broke above the "upper" scenario in four of twelve markets tested. And Bulgaria, the market this page singles out as the one where reversion is upside, is where the identical claim was most wrong two years ago: predicted +10.3%, actual −14.5%. Treat the three lines as a sensitivity range, not as bounds. Scenario A is not a ceiling and Scenario B is not a floor — which is why C is shown.

11 · LIMITATIONS

  • "Consolidation" is not measured anywhere in this study. Fewer, higher-yielding cows is consistent with fewer and larger farms, with the same farms culling low-yield animals, and with genetics — this data separates none of them. Eurostat publishes farm structure surveys (ef_lsk_main) that would settle it; they are not wired up. The word is avoided in this page's conclusions for that reason.
  • The veterinary line covers all livestock, so a per-head-of-cattle figure moves whenever a country's pig or poultry sector moves. It is used in section 6 for EU-level direction only, never for cross-country level comparison, and no pricing recommendation rests on a country's veterinary figure.
  • The United States is not in this addendum. No equivalent per-category inventory is wired up. Every figure here is EU or Türkiye.
  • Two methodology breaks are present and are not spanned by any published figure here. The Turkish herd steps +13.2% in 2016→2017. Polish dairy cows read 2,243k in 2023 against 2,037k in 2022 and 1,960k in 2024 — a probable revision artefact; no figure on this page uses 2023, and the 2015 and 2024 endpoints sit on trend.
  • EU non-dairy cows read 10,082.27 for 2024 and 10,082.29 for 2025 — twenty head apart in a ten-million-head population. The 2025 value is provisional or carried forward and is excluded here.
  • Milk yield is production ÷ dairy cows, not a herd-book yield, and is not adjusted for fat or protein content. Carcass weight per head is derived as slaughter tonnage ÷ head slaughtered × 1,000.
  • Output value is nominal euro deflated by euro-area HICP. For the eight markets outside the euro — Poland, Czechia, Denmark, Sweden, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and Croatia — Eurostat converts at market rates, so their value figures carry an FX component no deflator removes.
  • The value pool is producer revenue from cattle and milk (AM111000 + AM121000). It is not our addressable market, it is not netted of the 2022 feed and energy shock, and it is not split between beef and dairy — a split our portfolio is not indifferent to.
  • Small markets move in percentages that do not compare. Cyprus (84,000 head) and Malta (13,000) are shown and never ranked on, except where a stated range requires the true extreme.

12 · WHAT THIS STILL CANNOT ANSWER

  • Where our revenue actually is. This page ranks markets by producer economics, not by our exposure. If Poland is a small share of EU revenue and France and Germany are most of it, the ranking is decoration until it is weighted by our own book.
  • Beef or dairy. The pool sums live cattle and milk output. The split is computable from the same files and is not shown.
  • What our prices actually did in these markets over the same decade — without which "price to value" is untestable against our own history.
  • What producer costs did. The main study is a margin study; this addendum uses revenue and never nets off the 2022 input shock, though the feed line sits in the same Eurostat table.
  • The size of the prize in euro. Everything here is a percentage.
