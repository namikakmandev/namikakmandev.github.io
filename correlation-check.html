Correlation & Regression Check

Two completely unrelated random walks correlate at r = 0.96 with p = 10−68 — press Load the trap and watch it happen. Then paste your own two series and see whether your correlation survives the checks that catch it: levels against changes, sample size discounted for autocorrelation, confidence intervals, methodology breaks, and lag analysis corrected for how many lags you looked at. Then it fits the regression — the slope with honest errors, a confounder control if you give it a third column, residual diagnostics, and four robustness checks including out-of-sample validation. Everything runs in your browser — no data leaves the page.

How this checker works — the eight traps it tests for
  1. Shared trend. Any two series that both drift correlate for free. The checker recomputes r on period-to-period changes; if the correlation only exists in levels, it was two trends passing each other.
  2. Sample size that only looks large. 120 monthly observations of a slow-moving series are not 120 independent facts — each month is mostly last month. The checker discounts n for autocorrelation (Bartlett/Quenouille) and recomputes the p-value on that effective sample size.
  3. Methodology breaks. A single implausible step (a survey basis change, a redefinition) is flagged by median absolute deviation before you compare anything across it.
  4. Zero-crossing series. Percent change is undefined on a series that changes sign (margins, net flows). The checker detects this and switches to absolute differences.
  5. Fake leads. Scanning 13 lags and keeping the best is 13 tests, not one. The lag table applies a Bonferroni correction, and a peak barely above lag zero is called what it is: noise.
  6. A slope with dishonest errors. The regression is fitted on changes, and its standard errors are widened by the same effective-sample-size discount as the correlation — the slope cannot claim confidence the correlation was denied. Durbin–Watson flags autocorrelated residuals.
  7. Confounders. Give it a third column and it re-fits holding that variable constant. If the X coefficient collapses, X was a proxy — this is the question correlation alone can never answer.
  8. The one honest exception. When levels correlate strongly, an Engle–Granger test asks whether the two series are cointegrated — tied together long-run so that differencing would destroy a real relationship. A strong pass is reported as the exception; a bare 5% pass is labelled borderline, because 1 in 20 unrelated trending pairs also passes.
  9. Hunting through pairs. A session ledger counts every check you run and shows the Bonferroni-adjusted bar. Test twenty pairs and keep the best, and the honest threshold is 0.0025, not 0.05.
  10. Fragile findings. Spearman vs Pearson (nonlinearity), drop-one influence (one point moving the result), split-half agreement (a period, not a relationship), and out-of-sample R² — fit on the first 70%, scored on the last 30%, where a negative score means the model does worse than guessing the mean.

Statistics: Pearson r; two-sided p from the exact t distribution; 95% CI by Fisher z; a 2,000-draw permutation test as an assumption-free cross-check. The engine is validated against a reference implementation on published data.

    What each column means

    The same correlation is computed twice, on two forms of your data:

    1. Levels — the raw values exactly as you pasted them. Two series that both grow over time will correlate here even when they have nothing to do with each other — that is shared trend, not a relationship.
    2. Changes — each period’s move (percent change, or year-on-year if ticked). Differencing removes the trend, so what is left is genuine co-movement. When the two rows disagree, trust this one.

    And the columns, left to right:

    1. n — how many observations that form has.
    2. r — the Pearson correlation, from −1 to +1: how tightly the points hug a straight line. It says nothing about how steep the line is, and nothing about cause.
    3. R² — r squared: the share of one series’ variation the other accounts for. r = 0.5 sounds strong but explains only 25%.
    4. p (naive) — the chance of seeing an |r| this large by pure luck, if every row were an independent observation. In time series each month is mostly last month, so this assumption fails and the naive p flatters the result.
    5. n effective — how many genuinely independent observations the sample carries once that autocorrelation is discounted. 120 monthly readings of a slow-moving series can carry as few as 3.
    6. p (adjusted) — the same test recomputed on n effective. This is the p-value the verdict is based on.
    7. 95% CI — the range the true correlation plausibly lies in. If it straddles zero, the sign itself is not established.
    8. verdict — significant only when the adjusted p is below 0.05. A huge r with a spectacular naive p can still be not significant — that is the whole trap.
