America kept the sales. It gave away the factories.

Every decade a new device drove chip demand: mainframe memory, PCs, mobile phones, smartphones, now AI data centres. Through all five, the same split widened.

In 1990 the United States hosted 37% of the world’s chipmaking capacity. By 2022 it hosted 10%, and none of the capacity for the most advanced chips, below 10 nanometres. Yet in 2025 US companies took 53% of world chip sales, their highest share since 1984.

America kept the price and gave away the factory. Design, design software and the customer stayed home; the capital-heavy manufacturing moved to Taiwan and Korea. It worked, until the factory became the risk.

The sales line joins two surveys: North American firms in 1988 (UPI industry survey) and US-headquartered firms in 2025 (SIA). The capacity line is the US share of world wafer fabrication capacity. Measured data only, no forecasts. Regenerate with python3 scripts/chips_sales_factories.py.

1

Design stayed

Nvidia, Apple, AMD and Qualcomm design the chips and pay a foundry to make them. Design-only (fabless) firms worldwide grew from about 14% of world IC sales in 2003 to 34.8% in 2021.

2

Making left

In 2022, all capacity for logic chips below 10 nm sat in Taiwan (69%) and Korea (31%). China and Taiwan hold over 60% of world assembly and test capacity.

3

Except memory

Samsung and SK hynix design and build their own chips, so the split does not happen there. Korea holds about half of world DRAM capacity; only 3% of it is in the US.

Now America wants the factories back

2022, the carrot. The CHIPS and Science Act appropriated $52.7 billion for US semiconductor manufacturing, research and workforce, including $39 billion in manufacturing incentives.

2026, the stick. On 14 January 2026 a Section 232 proclamation put a 25% tariff on certain advanced computing chips imported into the US, naming Nvidia’s H200 and AMD’s MI325X as examples, with exemptions for imports that support building the US supply chain.

The pricing point: the first chips named are designed by US companies. America now taxes chips its own firms designed, to pull the factories home. Who absorbs that 25% — the designer, its customers, or the foundry — is a pricing question before it is a political one.

What this does not show

The two lines in the chart are different measures: one is where the factories are, the other is which companies collect the revenue. A US company’s chip made in Taiwan counts for the US on the sales line and for Taiwan on the capacity line; that gap is the point. The sales line joins two surveys with slightly different definitions (North American firms in 1988, US-headquartered firms in 2025). The decade labels for chip demand are industry history, not a measured series; the only measured end-market split used is 2023. No US share figure is published for each chip type at the design or sales step, so the interactive page shows those steps for all chips.
