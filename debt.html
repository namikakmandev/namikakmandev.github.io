Inflation paid down the rich world's debt. From here, interest adds to it.

Part one showed the yields. This part follows them into the debt. Between 2021 and 2025 nine rich-world governments cut their debt ratios, or held them, while running primary deficits over the five years taken together. Inflation did the work: it took 11 to 25 points of GDP off every one of the nine debt ratios. On the IMF's own projections that help shrinks to 2031. Interest becomes the largest force pushing debt up in six of the nine, and the IMF's path has the rate paid on the debt rising more slowly than history says it will. Thirty years of data say old debt reprices towards the market yield with a half-life of about five years. At today's yields and that speed, four of the nine are paying more on their debt than their economies grow by 2031. The IMF's path has one.

The yields, the US interest bill and the six-country debt and deficit chart are in part one and are not repeated here. Every figure below is recomputed from the committed data by scripts/debt_study.py, from the IMF World Economic Outlook and the OECD ten-year yields on FRED. The sources and caveats are listed at the end.

Who cut the debt: inflationChange in general government gross debt, points of GDP, summed over 2021–25 and over the IMF's 2026–31, split into its parts.

A debt ratio moves for five reasons: the primary deficit (the budget before interest) adds to it, interest adds to it, real growth shrinks it, inflation shrinks it, and a residual covers everything else, such as bank rescues, asset sales and exchange rates. Taken over 2021–25 as a whole, every one of the nine ran primary deficits (Italy's surpluses came only in 2024–25), adding 3 points of GDP to the Dutch ratio and 23 to the American. Real growth took some of that back. Inflation took more: 25 points off Japan's ratio, 25 off the United States', 20 off Italy's and Spain's, 19 off Britain's. Take the inflation bar away and every one of the nine ratios would have risen over the five years. In the IMF's projection to 2031, inflation's help per year falls in all nine, by between a fifth and a half, and interest is the largest force pushing debt up in six of the nine countries, ahead of the primary deficit. The exceptions are Germany, Japan and the Netherlands, whose primary deficits stay larger.

The bill, against revenue and the 1990sGeneral government net interest as a share of general government revenue, per cent, 1990 to 2031.

Measured against what governments collect rather than against GDP, the bill is larger. It is also the comparison that tells you whether the rich world has been here before. It has. In 1993 Italy spent a quarter of its revenue on interest, and in 1996 Spain spent an eighth. Today the United States spends 11.8%, twice its share of 2015 and by far the largest in the group, and the IMF has it at 14.0% by 2031, above Spain's 1990s mark. Britain, at 6.8%, is already above anything its 1990s figures show. France goes from 3.8% to 6.2% and passes its 1996 level. Japan goes from 0.6% to 4.9%, which is also above its late-1990s figure. Italy, at 7.5%, is less than a third of where it was.

imf-weo: (GGXONLB_NGDP − GGXCNL_NGDP) ÷ GGR_NGDP. The IMF's US general government series start in 2001. Dashed after 2025. Net interest, so the Japanese figure understates what Tokyo pays on its bonds.

The rate paid, against growthAverage rate paid on the debt (r) and nominal GDP growth (g), per cent a year, 2000 to 2031. The red line is the ten-year yield in August 2026.

Whether a debt ratio grows by itself depends on r − g, the gap between the average rate on the stock and nominal growth. In 2021–23 that gap averaged between −5.3 and −8.4 points in eight of the nine countries, as inflation raised nominal GDP and the stock kept its old coupons. By 2025 it had closed to −0.3 in France and turned positive, +0.2, in Italy. In every panel the red line, today's ten-year yield, sits above the average rate. That is the gap the average rate now has to close.

How fast the yield reaches the debtYearly change in the rate paid on the debt against the gap between the ten-year yield and last year's rate, 1996 to 2025, and where it leads by 2031.

The question part one could not answer is how quickly a higher yield turns into a higher interest bill. There is no maturity data here, but the speed can be measured from the result. Each year the average rate on the stock closes part of its gap to the ten-year yield. Pooled across eight countries over thirty years, the share closed is 13% a year (standard error 2.3 points), a half-life of five years. A check that removes the shared measurement error between the two sides of the regression gives a faster 17%, so five years is the slow end. Italy's debt reprices fastest, at a two-year half-life, the Netherlands' and Australia's slowest. Japan's measured speed is close to zero, because its central bank held the yield and its net interest is dominated by asset income, so it is left out of the pooled figure.

Apply that speed to today's yields and the IMF's 2031 rate on the debt is too low in all nine: by 0.8 points for the US, 1.0 for Britain, 1.9 for Australia, 0.8 for Japan and 0.1 to 0.3 for France, Italy, Germany, Spain and the Netherlands. The IMF may be assuming yields fall back. But if they stay where they are, the rate paid is above nominal growth in 2031 in the US, Britain, France and Italy, against Italy alone on the IMF's path. That is what "flirting" means in numbers: four of the nine, the largest economy among them, cross the line within five years unless yields come down.

What holding the ratio steady costsPrimary balance, per cent of GDP, in 2031: the IMF's projection, what holds the debt ratio at the IMF's rates, and what holds it if the whole stock paid today's ten-year yield.

With r close to g, a government holds its debt ratio by roughly balancing its budget before interest. At today's yields, every country but Spain needs a primary surplus to do it. Spain is also the only one whose projected budget covers the need, because the IMF has its nominal growth at 4.1%. Italy is 0.1 points of GDP short and Britain 0.4. The US is 4.6 points short, as its primary deficit of 3.1% in 2031 meets a required surplus of 1.5%. Japan is 2.7 short and Germany 2.4, as their primary deficits widen in the IMF's projection. On the debt ratio, Japan and Italy lead the group. On the budget it takes to hold the ratio steady, the US leads by a distance.

What to do with this

Supported"The rich world is flirting with a debt problem"
In numbers: the average rate is still at or below growth, the market rate is above it, and the average closes about 13% of that gap a year. At today's yields the US, Britain, France and Italy cross into r above g by 2031. "Unsustainable" is not supported yet.
What would change it: ten-year yields falling below the IMF's 2031 nominal growth, which is 2.7% for Italy and Japan, 2.8% for France and Germany, 3.2% for Britain and the Netherlands, 3.6% for the US.
Not supported"Governments paid down their pandemic debt"
Every one of the nine ran a primary deficit over 2021–25 taken as a whole. The ratios fell because inflation and growth raised GDP. Without the inflation term every one of the nine would have risen.
What would change it: nothing in the past data. For the future, the IMF has Italy, Britain, France and Spain moving to primary surpluses by 2031.
WatchAnyone reading the IMF's 2031 debt paths
Check the interest assumption. At the historical repricing speed and today's yields the IMF's 2031 rate on the debt is low by 0.8 points for the US and 1.0 for Britain. On US gross debt of 142% of GDP, 0.8 points is about 1.1% of GDP a year in interest.
What would change it: the October WEO, if it moves the rate paid up towards the market. The fetch picks up each new release.

Read with care

  • Net interest. Interest here is the primary balance minus the overall balance, so interest paid minus interest received. For the US, Italy, France, Britain and Spain the two are close. For Japan, Australia and the Netherlands, governments with large financial assets, net is well below gross. Canada and Korea are left out because their net interest is negative and their debt changes leave residuals of 14 to 30 points.
  • Net interest over gross debt understates the coupon. The net-debt version is not used because several countries' net debt is small or has definitional breaks.
  • The pass-through is one estimate. Ordinary least squares on 233 country-years, with a country intercept, of the yearly change in r on the gap between the annual-mean ten-year yield and last year's r. The per-country figures rest on 23 to 30 points and carry wide errors, which are printed. The ten-year yield stands in for the whole curve: governments also borrow in bills and in inflation-linked bonds, which reprice faster. Maturity has lengthened since the 1990s, which would slow repricing now.
  • "At today's yields" holds the August 2026 monthly average for six years. That is arithmetic to show where the numbers move, not a forecast. The IMF's projections embed their own rate path, which the WEO does not publish in this file.
  • Projections. The WEO file does not mark where actuals end. This page treats 2025 as the last actual year. 2026 to 2031 are the IMF's assumptions and are revised twice a year.
  • The inflation term uses the GDP deflator, not consumer prices, and it is computed from the nominal and real growth rates the IMF publishes. The residual absorbs anything the identity misses, and it is printed.
  • Not measured. Who holds the debt, central-bank balance sheets and their losses, maturity profiles, contingent liabilities. The page shows the arithmetic of the stock. It does not predict a crisis or its timing.
