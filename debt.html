Borrowing above growth: the rich world's debt stops shrinking itself

Through the 2010s five of the six big rich-world borrowers paid less on their debt than their economies grew, and in 2021–23 inflation made the gap enormous: nominal GDP outran the average interest rate by five to seven points a year, and debt ratios fell without anyone repaying anything. That gap has almost closed. In 2025 Italy already paid more on its debt than its economy grew; the IMF's own numbers put France, Britain and the United States within half a point of the line by 2031. And the rate the market charges on new ten-year money is above the IMF's 2031 growth rate in all six. The stock is still cheap because it was borrowed cheap. Every bond that matures is refinanced at a price growth does not cover.

A companion to Five per cent: the bond market reprices the state, which covers the yields. This page covers what the yields do to the stock: the interest bill, the rate paid on the debt against nominal growth, and the budget each government would need to hold its debt ratio steady. Every figure is recomputed from the committed data by scripts/debt_study.py; the sources and caveats are listed at the end.

The stockGeneral government gross debt, per cent of GDP, 1995 to 2025 actual and IMF projections to 2031 (dashed). IMF World Economic Outlook.

Debt ratios jumped in 2020 and then fell. The fall was mostly not paid for: inflation raised nominal GDP faster than the interest bill, which the next sections show. From here the IMF has the US ratio rising every year, from 124% to 142% of GDP, and Germany's from 63% to 74%. France rises to 121%. Britain and Italy stay flat, and Japan's falls, though from 207%.

imf-weo: GGXWDG_NGDP. Actuals to 2025, IMF projections after (dashed). US general government, so about 25 points above the federal debt held by the public.

The billGeneral government net interest, per cent of GDP: primary balance minus overall balance, 1995 to 2031. IMF World Economic Outlook.

Interest is the part of the deficit that does not respond to policy this year. The US paid 3.65% of GDP in 2025, the most in the IMF's series, which starts in 2001, and the IMF has it at 4.3% by 2031. Italy is at the same level and rises alongside, to 4.3%. The steeper climbs are in the countries whose bills were smallest: France's has gone from 1.3% of GDP in 2020 to 2.0%, and the IMF has it at 3.3% by 2031. Britain's has more than doubled since 2020, from 1.1% to 2.6%. Japan's rises from 0.2% to 1.7%, which is more than eightfold, though the Japanese figure is net of large government asset income and understates what Tokyo pays on its bonds.

imf-weo: GGXONLB_NGDP − GGXCNL_NGDP. That is interest paid minus interest received, so for governments with large financial assets (Japan) it is well below gross interest paid.

The rate paid, against growthAverage rate paid on the debt (r) and nominal GDP growth (g), per cent a year, 2000 to 2031. The horizontal line is the ten-year yield in August 2026.

What decides whether a debt ratio grows by itself is the gap between the average rate on the stock and nominal growth, r − g. When it is negative a government can run a small primary deficit, meaning its budget before interest, and still watch the ratio fall. In 2015–19 it averaged between −0.7 and −2.2 points everywhere except Italy, which was already at +0.75. In 2021–23 it averaged between −5 and −7 points in five of the six countries, as inflation pushed up nominal GDP while the stock kept its old coupons. In 2025 it was back at −0.3 in France and +0.2 in Italy. The dashed lines are the IMF's: r rises in every country as old bonds mature, g drifts down to between 2.7% and 3.6%, and by 2031 the gap is between −0.7 and +0.6 points everywhere except Japan.

The horizontal line is the part the IMF's path does not show directly. It is what a new ten-year bond costs today, and in all six countries it is above the growth rate the IMF expects in 2031: by 1.8 points in Britain, 1.1 to 1.3 in Italy, France and the US, and a few tenths in Germany and Japan. The average rate climbs towards the new rate as the stock rolls over, whatever the IMF assumes.

What holding the ratio steady costsPrimary balance (the budget before interest), per cent of GDP, in 2031: what the IMF projects, what would hold debt steady at the IMF's rates, and what would hold it at today's yields.

With r − g near zero, a government holds its debt ratio steady by roughly balancing its budget before interest. Italy already does: a primary surplus of 0.5% of GDP in 2025, rising to 1.6% in the IMF's projection, which is why its ratio is flat despite the highest rate in the group. The US does not come close. Its primary deficit is 3.2% of GDP in 2025 and 3.1% in 2031, when 0.5% would hold the ratio at the IMF's rates. The last columns do the same sum with the whole stock paying today's ten-year yield instead. This is arithmetic, not a forecast: it is where the requirement moves if yields stay where they are for a decade. On that basis every one of the six needs a primary surplus, and the gap between the IMF's projected budget and the one needed is 4.6 points of GDP for the US, 2.7 for Japan, 2.4 for Germany, 1.0 for France, 0.4 for Britain and 0.1 for Italy.

What to do with this

SupportedAnyone writing that the rich world is flirting with a debt problem
Say it this way: the average rate is still at or below growth, the marginal rate is above it, and the average is moving towards the marginal. That is what the data support. "Debt is unsustainable" is not, yet, in five of the six countries.
What would change it: ten-year yields falling below the IMF's 2031 nominal growth, which is 2.7% for Italy and Japan, 2.8% for France and Germany, 3.2% for Britain and 3.6% for the US.
WatchAnyone ranking which country is most exposed
Rank by the gap between the projected primary balance and the one needed, not by the debt ratio. On debt, Japan and Italy lead. On the gap, the US leads by a distance and Italy is the smallest.
What would change it: a US budget that moves the primary balance towards zero. The IMF's current projection assumes none.
Not supported"Inflation will take care of the debt"
It did, once: 2021–23 had r − g of −5 to −7 points. It worked because the inflation was a surprise and the stock was locked in at old coupons. Markets now price inflation into new bonds, and inflation-linked and short-dated debt reprice straight away.
What would change it: another round of inflation the bond market does not see coming. By construction that cannot be planned for.

Read with care

  • Net, not gross, interest. The IMF publishes the primary balance, and interest here is the primary balance minus the overall balance: interest paid minus interest received. For the US, Italy, France and Britain the two are close. For Japan they are not, because the government earns large income on its financial assets. Canada is left out for the same reason: its net interest is negative.
  • Net interest over gross debt. The "rate paid" divides net interest by gross debt, so it understates the coupon, most for Japan and Germany. The net-debt version is in data/debt-study.json as r_net_2025. It is higher, but it tells the same story.
  • Projections. The WEO file does not mark where actuals end. This page treats 2025 as the last actual year and draws 2026 to 2031 dashed. Those years are the IMF's fiscal and growth assumptions and are revised twice a year.
  • The "at today's yields" columns apply one number, the August 2026 ten-year monthly average, to the whole stock, and set it against the IMF's 2031 growth. That is an identity, used to show where the requirement moves. Governments borrow across the curve, in bills and in inflation-linked bonds, and the stock reprices over years. The average maturity of the debt, which sets that pace, is not in the data here.
  • Growth is nominal. g includes inflation, which is why it spiked in 2021–23. A fall in inflation lowers g straight away and lowers r only slowly, which is the risk this page describes.
  • Not measured. Who holds the debt, central-bank balance sheets and their losses, maturity profiles, and contingent liabilities such as pensions and banks. The page shows the arithmetic of the stock. It does not predict a crisis or its timing.
  • Reproduce it. python3 scripts/debt_study.py rewrites data/debt-study.json from data/imf-weo.json (GGXWDG_NGDP, GGXWDN_NGDP, GGXCNL_NGDP, GGXONLB_NGDP, NGDP) and data/long-yields.json.
