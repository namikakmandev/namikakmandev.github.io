Concept 1 · Stationarity & the unit root

Two walks uphill

Why two series that both drift will look related even when nothing connects them — and how one test tells the difference. Built and worked on the four-market cattle parity data.

Station 01

The machine that manufactures relationships

Below are two series with nothing whatsoever connecting them. Each month, each one takes a random step up or down. No shared cause, no season, no hidden driver. Pure coin-flips.

Watch the correlation between them as they walk. Run it a few times. Most runs end somewhere well away from zero — and a good share end above 0.5, which any standard test would call a strong relationship.

Live · two independent random walks 200 months, one step per frame
series A series B each one's own average
r = 0.000CORRELATION SO FAR

Nothing is being faked here. The correlation is real arithmetic on the numbers. It is the drifting itself that manufactures it: each walk spends long stretches on one side of its own average, and two walks doing that at the same time is not rare — it is the normal case.

This is not the ice-cream-and-drowning problem. There, a real third cause exists — summer heat — and you have a genuine finding you have misread. Here there is no third cause to find. The relationship does not exist at all, and no amount of thinking about hidden drivers will reveal that.

Station 03

One number decides everything: ρ

Write this month's value as a function of last month's. That single equation, and one coefficient inside it, separates a series with a home from a series without one.

yt  =  c  +  ρ · yt−1  +  εt yt — this month · yt−1 — last month · εt — this month's random shock · c — a constant

ρ (rho) is how much of last month survives into this month. Drag it. The dashed line is the series' home level; watch whether the line is pulled back to it or drifts away and forgets it.

Below about 0.9 the series is tethered — shocks fade, the line keeps crossing home. Push ρ to 1.000 and the equation becomes yt = c + yt−1 + εt: this month is last month plus a shock, forever. Every shock becomes permanent. The series never returns, because there is no longer anywhere to return to.

ρ = 1 is called a unit root — literally, the root of the equation equals one. A series with a unit root is a random walk, and a random walk is the machine from Station 01.

So the whole question — can I read this level, or only its change? — reduces to one measurement: is ρ equal to 1, or less than 1?

Station 04

How the test actually measures it

Testing ρ = 1 directly is awkward. Dickey and Fuller's move in 1979 was to subtract yt−1 from both sides, which turns the question into an ordinary one about a coefficient being zero.

yt − yt−1  =  c  +  (ρ − 1) · yt−1  +  εt
Δyt  =  c  +  γ · yt−1  +  εt γ = ρ − 1  ·  so ρ = 1 becomes γ = 0

Read γ as a pull-back force. It answers: when the level sits high, does next month's change tend to be negative? If yes, something is pulling the series home. If γ is zero, the level tells you nothing about the next move — no anchor exists.

Here is that regression on your own series: US parity, 667 months. Each dot is one month — its position on the horizontal axis is where parity stood, and on the vertical axis what happened next.

The slope tilts down: γ = −0.0284. About 2.8% of any gap closes each month — a real anchor, but a slow one, which is why the half-life came out near two years.

The trap almost everyone falls into

You now have t = −3.36 and the instinct is to compare it against the usual t-table, where the 5% cut-off is about −1.65. That comparison is invalid. When ρ is near 1, this statistic does not follow the t-distribution at all. Dickey and Fuller derived its true distribution by simulation, and it sits much further left.

Same statistic, two rulers why you cannot read this off a standard table

Against the correct ruler the US series still clears the bar — but by a margin, not a mile. Under the wrong ruler it would have looked overwhelming. The regression is ordinary; the critical values are not.

Station 05

Your data: what the level was hiding

Now the same idea on the actual series behind the study. Each dot is one month: US slaughter cattle against US corn, 1971 to 2026.

Start on Levels — the prices themselves. Then switch to Changes and watch every dot move to its month-on-month position. The cloud is the same 666 months either way. Only the question changes.

US cattle vs corn · 666 months producer price indexes, FRED/BLS
r = 0.587CORRELATION

In levels the dots form a rising band and the correlation reads 0.587. In changes the band collapses into a cloud and the correlation falls to 0.083. Nothing was removed — the same months, the same numbers. The 0.587 was mostly two prices climbing through the same fifty years.

Your deck already says it: “one ratio, read as change — never as level.” That line was written from experience. This is the same statement with a measurement behind it.

Station 06

The test that should worry you

Turkish meat prices against two things: Turkish feed prices — the real relationship your study is built on — and the share price of Eli Lilly, an American pharmaceutical company. Same window, same months.

One of these pairs is connected. The other is beef and Ozempic. In levels, see whether you can tell which is which.

0.990 against 0.983. In levels, your genuine relationship and a completely absurd one are almost indistinguishable. That number cannot separate them, so it cannot be evidence for either.

Switch to changes and they part company immediately: meat and feed hold at 0.512 — a real, moderate link — while Lilly collapses to 0.108, correctly reading as nothing. Turkey's level correlation is doing two jobs at once: hiding a real relationship and flattering a fake one, both because everything in that window is climbing on Turkish inflation.

Only the US rejects — and it rejects on ADF while also being rejected by KPSS, whose null is the opposite. Both tests firing is not a contradiction to sweep up; it is a specific diagnosis: a series whose home level moved partway through the sample rather than one that either sits still or wanders freely.

Which is exactly what turned up in September 2006, when the ethanol build-out changed what corn was for. But that belongs to concept 3.

What this means for the study, in one line each.

“Parity is high against its own average” is a sentence only the US series can support, and only before 2006. Everywhere else there is no established average to be high against — so the honest phrasing is the direction of travel, not the standing.
