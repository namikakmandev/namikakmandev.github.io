Why subtract the average

Every measurement in this study — the correlation, the slope, the ADF statistic — starts by turning each number into its distance from the average. Here is what that step does, and what happens if you skip it.

Station 01

What raw numbers cannot tell you

Three sets of six points. In the first, y climbs as x climbs. In the second, y falls as x climbs — the opposite. In the third there is no pattern at all.

Now multiply x by y for each point and add them up, using the numbers exactly as they are. Press Measure from zero.

Measured from zero, all three totals come out large and positive — 348, 268, 288. The rising set and the falling set, which are opposites, produce almost the same answer. The measurement is useless: it is mostly reporting that all the numbers happen to be positive.

Switch to measuring from the average and the same three sets give +40, −40 and −6. Now the rising set and the falling set are exact opposites, and the patternless one sits near zero. Same data, same multiplication — only the starting point moved.

That is the whole reason. Raw values answer “how big is this number?” Distances from the average answer “is this number unusually high or unusually low?” — and only the second question can reveal a pattern.

Station 02

The crosshair, and the four corners it makes

Draw a vertical line at the average x, and a horizontal line at the average y. That crosshair splits the picture into four corners — and the corner a point falls into is the whole story.

Drag any dot. Watch its corner change, its contribution flip sign, and the total move.

Each point contributes (x − x̄) × (y − ȳ). Two negatives multiply to a positive, so a point that is low on both counts exactly like one that is high on both — both are evidence the two move together. A point high on one and low on the other counts against.

Add the six products and you get the total. Divide that total by the spread of x and you have the slope. Divide it by both spreads and you have the correlation. Two different questions, one shared sum.

344 months pulled back toward the middle; 322 pushed further out. That is a thin majority — not a dramatic one. And that thinness is the shallow slope: a pull that exists, but only just.

The sum of all 666 products comes to −2.15. Negative, because the pull-back corners outweigh the others. Divide by the spread of the horizontal axis and you get −0.0284 — the γ from the main page, the 2.8% of the gap that closes each month.

Nothing new was introduced here. The ADF statistic, the slope and the correlation are all built from this one picture: count each month by which corner it lands in, weight it by how far out it sits, add them up.

Station 04

In a spreadsheet

Nothing here needs software. With x in column A and y in column B:

C2 = A2 − AVERAGE($A$2:$A$100)
D2 = B2 − AVERAGE($B$2:$B$100)
E2 = C2 * D2

total = SUM(E2:E100) slope = total ÷ SUM(C2:C100^2)  ·  or just SLOPE(B:B, A:A)
correlation = CORREL(A:A, B:B)

Column C answers “how unusual was x that month.” Column D, the same for y. Column E multiplies them, so it is positive when the two were unusual in the same direction and negative when they were not. Everything downstream is that column, added up and rescaled.
