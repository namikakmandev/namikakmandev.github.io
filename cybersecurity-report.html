01Executive Summary

Cybercrime keeps growing, but its center of gravity has not moved: people. According to the Verizon 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report (DBIR), the human element — social engineering, phishing, stolen credentials, simple mistakes — was present in 62% of all confirmed data breaches, up from 60% a year earlier.[1] The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) recorded more than $20.8 billion in reported cybercrime losses in 2025, a 26% jump in a single year, and phishing was once again the most reported crime type.[2]

The financial stakes are highest in the United States: IBM's Cost of a Data Breach Report puts the average cost of a US data breach at a record $10.22 million — more than double the global average of $4.44 million.[3] Meanwhile the Identity Theft Resource Center counted a record 3,322 data compromises in the US in 2025, a 79% rise over five years.[4]

The conclusion of this briefing is simple: technology alone does not stop breaches. A trained, alert person — the "human firewall" — is the single most cost-effective security control an organization can invest in. 02Information Security: The Fundamentals

Keyword: information security

Information security is the practice of protecting information — digital or physical — from unauthorized access, alteration, or destruction. The discipline is traditionally organized around three goals, known as the CIA triad:

  • Confidentiality — only authorized people can see the information (think: encryption, access controls).
  • Integrity — the information cannot be tampered with undetected (think: checksums, audit logs).
  • Availability — the information is there when legitimate users need it (think: backups, redundancy).

The most widely used blueprint for organizing this work is the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, published by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology in 2024. It structures a security program into six functions — Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover — and is deliberately written so that small businesses and beginners can apply it, not just large enterprises.[5]

Why it matters for everyone

Information security is no longer an IT-department topic. With 62% of breaches involving a human action, every employee who opens email or handles customer data is part of the security perimeter — whether they know it or not.[1]

03The Data Breach Landscape in 2025–2026

Keyword: data breach

A data breach is a confirmed incident in which sensitive or protected data is accessed or disclosed without authorization. The scale in the United States is documented every year by two respected sources: the Identity Theft Resource Center (ITRC) and IBM.

The ITRC's 2025 Annual Data Breach Report counted 3,322 data compromises — the highest ever recorded and a 79% increase over five years. Roughly 279 million victim notices were issued in 2025; that number was actually far lower than 2024's 1.37 billion, only because 2024 was inflated by a handful of "mega-breaches."[4] In other words: fewer giant single events, but more breaches overall.

On the cost side, IBM's Cost of a Data Breach Report 2025 (research by Ponemon Institute) found the global average breach cost fell 9% to $4.44 million — the first decline in five years, driven by faster AI-assisted detection. But the US moved in the opposite direction, hitting a record $10.22 million per breach, pushed up by regulatory penalties and slower detection. Healthcare remained the most expensive industry at $7.42 million per breach.[3]

Two more findings from the 2025–2026 research cycle deserve attention:

  • Attackers exploit unpatched software faster than defenders fix it. Vulnerability exploitation became the top initial access vector at 31% of breaches, while the median time to patch rose from 32 to 43 days.[1]
  • AI cuts both ways. IBM found attackers used AI (mainly AI-generated phishing and deepfakes) in 16% of breaches, and unsanctioned "shadow AI" tools were involved in 20% — while defenders using security AI detected and contained breaches in 241 days on average, a nine-year best.[3]

04Social Engineering: Hacking People, Not Computers

Keyword: social engineering

Social engineering is the manipulation of people into giving up information or access — no malware required. It works because it exploits trust, urgency, fear and authority rather than technical flaws. It is also the reason the "human element" statistic stays so stubbornly high.

The main techniques to recognize

  • Phishing — fraudulent emails impersonating a trusted brand or colleague. With roughly 192,000 complaints, phishing/spoofing was the single most reported cybercrime to the FBI in 2025 — more than double the second-place category — and reported phishing losses tripled to $215.8 million.[2]
  • Spear phishing & BEC — targeted attacks on a specific person, often finance staff, to trigger fraudulent wire transfers (Business Email Compromise).
  • Smishing & vishing — phishing by SMS or phone call. Verizon found the success rate of mobile social engineering rose about 40% year over year.[1]
  • Pretexting — inventing a believable scenario ("I'm from IT, I need your password to fix your account").
  • AI-assisted deception — deepfaked voices and AI-written lures. The FBI logged over 22,000 complaints referencing AI in 2025, with nearly $900 million in associated losses.[2]
The tell-tale signs
  • Unexpected urgency: "act in the next 30 minutes."
  • A request to bypass normal process (gift cards, secret wire transfers, "don't tell anyone").
  • Sender address or link domain that is slightly wrong.
  • Requests for credentials, MFA codes, or personal data — legitimate organizations do not ask for these by email or phone.

05Security Awareness: Building the Human Firewall

Keyword: security awareness

If 62% of breaches involve a human action, then training humans is not a "soft" measure — it is a frontline defense. Security awareness means giving every person the knowledge and reflexes to recognize an attack and to report it quickly. The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) distills personal cyber hygiene into four steps in its national Secure Our World program:[6]

1.Use strong passwords

Long, unique passwords for every account, stored in a password manager. NIST guidance favors length (passphrases) over forced complexity and frequent rotation.[7]

2.Turn on multifactor authentication

MFA blocks the vast majority of account-takeover attempts, because a stolen password alone is no longer enough.

3.Recognize and report phishing

Pause before clicking. Verify unexpected requests through a second channel, and report suspicious messages — reporting protects everyone else.

4.Update your software

Enable automatic updates. With exploited vulnerabilities now the top breach entry point, patching promptly is personal security, too.[1]

What good awareness programs share
  • Short, frequent training beats one annual slideshow.
  • Simulated phishing exercises with feedback — not punishment.
  • A no-blame reporting culture: the fastest-contained breaches are the ones reported early.
  • Leadership participates: security tone is set at the top (the "Govern" function in NIST CSF 2.0).[5]

06Data Protection: A Practical Checklist

Keyword: data protection

Data protection is the set of practices that keeps personal and business data safe across its whole lifecycle — collection, storage, use, sharing and deletion. The consumer stakes are documented by the Federal Trade Commission: Americans reported losing $12.5 billion to fraud in 2024 (up 25% in a year) and, per FTC testimony, nearly $16 billion in 2025, alongside more than a million identity-theft reports a year.[8]

For organizations

  • Know your data: inventory what personal data you hold, where it lives, and who can access it (NIST CSF "Identify").[5]
  • Minimize: collect only what you need, keep it only as long as you need it.
  • Encrypt sensitive data at rest and in transit; back up critical data offline.
  • Limit access by role (least privilege) and remove access when people leave.
  • Patch fast: prioritize known-exploited vulnerabilities — only 26% were fully remediated in 2025.[1]
  • Govern AI use: shadow AI appeared in 20% of breaches — set clear rules for what data may enter AI tools.[3]
  • Prepare an incident response plan before you need it (NIST CSF "Respond" and "Recover").[5]

For individuals

  • Follow CISA's four steps above — they cover most personal risk.[6]
  • Freeze your credit with the three bureaus; it is free and blocks most new-account identity theft.
  • Check whether your accounts appeared in known breaches, and change reused passwords first.
  • If you are a victim, use the FTC's IdentityTheft.gov for a personal recovery plan, and report cybercrime to the FBI at ic3.gov.[8]

07Key Takeaways

  1. People are the perimeter. 62% of breaches involve the human element — awareness training is a security control, not a formality.[1]
  2. The US pays the highest price. $10.22M per breach and $20.8B in annual reported cybercrime losses mean prevention is dramatically cheaper than response.[2][3]
  3. Social engineering is industrialized — and now AI-assisted. Verification habits ("trust, but verify through a second channel") defeat most of it.[1][2]
  4. Basics beat gadgets. Strong unique passwords, MFA, prompt updates and phishing reporting — CISA's four steps — would prevent a large share of real-world incidents.[6]
  5. Frameworks make it manageable. NIST CSF 2.0's six functions give even a small team a complete, credible roadmap.[5]
