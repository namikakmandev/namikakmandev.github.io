When prices topple governments

In 2024, every governing party in the rich democracies that held an election lost vote share. That had not happened once in more than 100 years of records.

The reason was not inflation. It was prices. Inflation is how fast prices rise. The price level is how high they already are. When inflation falls from 9% to 3%, nothing gets cheaper; prices just keep climbing more slowly from a much higher base. Voters don’t shop with inflation rates. They shop with prices.

Spacing on the timeline is not to scale. Each entry is a case where high prices were a main reason, not the only one.

Eight price shocks

1846, Britain — bread. Dear bread forced the end of the grain tariffs (the Corn Laws). The fight split the ruling party and the government fell.

1923, Germany — money printing. Hyperinflation. The cabinet resigned in August.

1970, Poland — food at state-set prices. Meat about +18% just before Christmas. Strikes followed, and the party leader was gone within a week.

1989, Venezuela — petrol. Petrol +100% and higher bus fares. Deadly riots in Caracas; the government survived.

1998, Indonesia — fuel. Fuel up to +70%. The president resigned after 32 years.

1998, India — onions. Onions +600% in three months. Delhi’s government lost its election, losing most seats where onion prices rose most. Consumer inflation reached 19.7% in November 1998, the month of the vote.

2023, Argentina — everything. 143% annual inflation in the last official figure before the runoff. Voters threw out the governing coalition.

2026, United States — petrol. $4.12 a gallon on 3 November, the highest petrol price on any Election Day. The president’s party lost both houses of Congress.

The gap: a quiet era, 1945–1971

From the end of the Second World War to about 1971, price-driven upheavals were rare in the West. Several things held prices still at once: wartime rationing and price controls, the Bretton Woods system tying currencies to the dollar and the dollar to gold, cheap oil until 1973, and a postwar boom in which wages grew faster than prices. Behind the Iron Curtain prices were set by the state, which froze the anger rather than removing it — Poland 1970 is what happened when the state raised them.

In 1971 the United States cut the dollar’s link to gold. The 1970s brought oil shocks and high inflation, and the price revolts came back.

Was it the system that kept prices calm, or just postwar growth? And could anything like it work today?

What this does not show

This is a set of cases, not a statistical test. Each was chosen because high prices were a main reason a government fell, lost an election or faced the streets (in Venezuela 1989 it survived); elections and uprisings always have more than one cause, and plenty of price shocks did not topple anyone. The price measures differ by entry: one staple (bread, meat, petrol, onions) in some, headline inflation in others. No leaders or parties are named.
